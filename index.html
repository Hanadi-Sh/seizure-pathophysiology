Scene 02

Normal Brain Activity → Seizure

Balanced excitatory and inhibitory neuronal firing maintains normal cerebral function. A seizure represents a sudden electrical disturbance in the cortex.

● Normal, excitatory (orange) and inhibitory (blue) balanced
Cerebral cortex, coronal view
Normal state: Neurons fire in varied, asynchronous patterns. Excitatory (glutamatergic) neurons (~80%, shown in orange) and inhibitory (GABAergic) neurons (~20%, shown in blue) are balanced, allowing organised cortical processing.

Seizure onset: A sudden cortical electrical disturbance causes neurons to fire excessively rapidly, rhythmically, and synchronously. Both populations are overwhelmed, and all neurons turn orange as hypersynchronised excitatory activity overrides normal inhibitory control and produces the clinical manifestations of a seizure.
Scene 03

Neurotransmitters: Excitation vs Inhibition

The balance between glutamate-mediated excitation and GABA-mediated inhibition is critical. Disruption of this balance underlies seizure generation.

GLUTAMATE: excitatory
GABA: inhibitory
Glutamate is the principal excitatory neurotransmitter. It binds to NMDA and AMPA receptors, opening Na⁺ (red) and Ca²⁺ (green) channels, causing depolarisation and increased neuronal firing.

GABA (γ-aminobutyric acid) is the principal inhibitory neurotransmitter. It binds to GABAA receptors, opening Cl⁻ (teal) channels, causing hyperpolarisation and reduced excitability.

Seizure implication: Excess excitation or reduced inhibition tips the balance toward seizure activity. Antiseizure medications work by enhancing GABAergic inhibition or reducing glutamatergic excitation.
⚡
Glutamate → Na⁺ / Ca²⁺ influx → depolarisation → increased firing
🛡
GABA → Cl⁻ influx → hyperpolarisation → reduced excitability
💊
Antiseizure drugs restore excitation and inhibition balance
Scene 04

Paroxysmal Depolarising Shift & Network Spread

Individual neurons undergo prolonged depolarisation (PDS), recruiting neighbouring neurons into a widening abnormal network.

Paroxysmal Depolarising Shift (PDS)
Click anywhere to trigger seizure focus
During a seizure, individual neurons undergo a paroxysmal depolarising shift (PDS), a prolonged, high-amplitude depolarisation with rapid repetitive firing.

This abnormal electrical activity spreads to nearby neurons, recruiting previously normal cells into the abnormal network. The pattern and extent of spread determines the clinical features (e.g. motor, sensory, autonomic manifestations).

The seizure terminates when inhibitory mechanisms, including GABAergic surround inhibition and neuronal exhaustion, overcome the excitatory drive.

🔗

⏹
Seizure stops when inhibition overcomes excitation
Scene 05

Epileptogenesis

The long-term process by which the brain becomes prone to seizures, from initial insult to chronic epilepsy.

Step 1: brain insult, excitability increases
Step 1, initial insult: After a brain insult (trauma, stroke, infection) or genetic disturbance, neurons gradually become more excitable and inhibitory control may decrease.

Step 2, network formation: Abnormal neurons can cause surrounding normal neurons to become hyperexcitable, forming an epileptic network.

Step 3, spontaneous seizures: Once this network becomes large enough, spontaneous seizures occur, leading to epilepsy.

Step 4, progressive damage: Repeated seizures may further damage the cortex and increase seizure susceptibility.
Scene 06

Genetic Factors

Understanding the role of genetics in epilepsy, acquired versus inherited causes.

Most patients can be reassured about family risk
Acquired does not mean inherited: Epilepsy caused by acquired factors (e.g. head trauma or stroke) is not inherited.

Inherited epilepsy: Is more commonly associated with generalised-onset seizures and develops during childhood.

Complex inheritance: Inheritance is usually complex and likely involves multiple abnormal genes (polygenic), each contributing a small effect.

Reassurance: Most patients can be reassured that their children and siblings are unlikely to develop epilepsy.

Key Highlights

  • Sudden cortical electrical disturbance initiates seizures
  • Excessively rapid, rhythmic, synchronised neuronal activity
  • Glutamate increases excitation; GABA reduces excitability
  • Paroxysmal depolarising shift drives abnormal firing
  • Brain insult or genetic disturbance increases excitability and decreases inhibition
  • Abnormal neurons recruit normal neighbours into an epileptic network
  • Once the network is large enough, spontaneous seizures occur (epilepsy)
  • Repeated seizures further damage cortex and increase susceptibility
  • Acquired epilepsy is not inherited; inherited forms are generalised and childhood-onset
  • Inheritance is polygenic, and most families can be reassured of low risk
